\chapter{Theoretical Background and Related Work}\label{ch:background}

The introduction framed the thesis as an evidence-design problem: a foundation
model should explain a live training state only through evidence that the
runtime can observe, validate, and replay. This chapter therefore does not
serve as a general survey of VR, aircraft simulation, or foundation models.
It reviews the closest lines of work through one question: why is an
evidence-grounded tutoring loop needed for procedural VR simulation?

The answer is cumulative. Manuals and checklists provide authority but not
live state. Intelligent tutoring systems provide task models but are usually
not built around foundation-model generation in a multimodal simulator.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds text but does not by itself know
whether the cockpit is in the claimed state. AR/VR guidance keeps instructions
in the task context, but much of it is authored or static. Vision-language
models (VLMs) can read display images, but their outputs must be converted into
bounded visual facts before they can influence a tutor. The contribution of
this thesis is positioned at the intersection of those gaps.

\section{Procedural Training in Immersive Simulation}\label{sec:bg-procedural}

Procedural training differs from factual instruction because success depends on
ordered action under state constraints. A learner must know not only what an
action is, but when it is permissible, how to check that it has taken effect,
and how to recover when an intermediate state differs from expectation. In
aviation and industrial settings, these requirements are usually externalised
through manuals, checklists, instructors, and simulator procedures. Flight
simulation has a long training tradition, but simulator effectiveness depends
on the task, feedback, and instructional design rather than on fidelity alone
\citep{hays1992flight}.

The cold-start task used in this thesis is a good stress case for such
instruction. It is compact enough to study in a master's thesis, yet it is
sequence-sensitive, state-dependent, and visually mediated. In DCS World, the
learner operates an F/A-18C Lot~20 through a Meta Quest~3 headset. The task
requires electrical power-up, engine start, display use, navigation alignment,
flight-control checks, and final pre-taxi configuration
\citep{dcsworld,eagledynamics2023hornetguide}. A paper checklist can describe
these steps, but an interactive tutor has to know which step is currently
actionable and which evidence supports that decision.

Virtual reality increases the cost of weak assistance. A learner in a headset
has less tolerance for switching to a PDF, searching a desktop window, or
replaying an external video. AR guidance for assembly and maintenance shows
the value of placing instructions inside the user's task context
\citep{tang2003arassembly,henderson2011ar,palmarini2018ar}. This thesis
adopts that in-context motivation, but adds a stricter systems condition:
generated help must be grounded in the current simulator state, not only
placed visually near the cockpit. Work on HMD-based and immersive VR learning
similarly cautions that instructional design, task fit, cognitive load, and
evaluation design determine whether immersion helps rather than distracts
\citep{jensen2018vrhmd,radianti2020ivr,makransky2021camil}.

\section{Existing Assistance Approaches}\label{sec:bg-existing}

\paragraph{Manuals, checklists, and simulator training.}
The most authoritative sources for the case study are the DCS World simulator,
the F/A-18C manual/training material, and the authored procedure pack derived
from them. These sources define the procedural prior. They specify which
actions matter and which state transitions should occur. Their limitation is
that they do not automatically observe the learner's current state. A checklist
line can be correct in the abstract while being wrong for the moment if a
precondition has not been met or if the learner has already passed the state
being described.

\paragraph{Intelligent and constraint-based tutoring.}
Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) research has long treated explicit task
models, learner-state tracking, feedback, and constraint-based diagnosis as
central to effective tutoring
\citep{vanlehn2011relative,kulik2016its,mitrovic2012constraint,aleven2009example}.
This is directly relevant to the prototype: a tutor needs more than conversational
ability; it needs a representation of the task and the learner's progress. The
difference is the operating substrate. The present system is not a classic ITS
with a closed problem interface. It observes a high-fidelity simulator through
telemetry, cockpit display images, authored gates, and event logs while using
foundation models as bounded components.

\paragraph{RAG and grounded language-model assistance.}
RAG conditions language generation on retrieved documents rather than relying
only on parametric memory \citep{lewis2020rag}. In education, large language
models can generate flexible explanations and feedback, but they also risk
unsupported confidence, hallucinated references, and fluent but context-free
answers \citep{kasneci2023chatgpt,ji2023hallucination}. Faithfulness work in
neural generation similarly shows that fluent generated text can diverge from
its source evidence \citep{maynez2020faithfulness}. Grounded action work such
as SayCan separates linguistic plausibility from action feasibility
\citep{ahn2022saycan}. The prototype follows the same design instinct: a response
may be fluent and sourced, but it should not be shown as procedural guidance
unless the runtime can validate the state, step, evidence references, and
overlay target.

\paragraph{AR/VR guidance and simulator telemetry.}
AR/VR procedural guidance addresses the interaction problem of keeping help in
the task context \citep{palmarini2018ar}. Simulator telemetry addresses a
different problem: observing state. In this thesis, an external telemetry
interface is used only as an input channel for raw simulator state. The thesis
does not claim that interface itself as an implementation contribution. The
contribution starts after raw telemetry enters the tutoring runtime: the system
selects relevant variables, maps raw controls to procedure-level predicates,
distinguishes source-missing from observed-false values, combines telemetry
with visual facts and procedure gates, and logs the resulting evidence for
replay.

\section{Why Existing Approaches Are Insufficient Here}\label{sec:bg-gap}

The case study requires four properties at once. First, assistance must be
live-state aware. A system that only retrieves the right manual paragraph can
still recommend the wrong action if the simulator state has not reached the
required precondition. Second, assistance must be multimodal. Raw telemetry
covers many switch positions and indicators, but cockpit display pages and text cues
also matter. The left and right Digital Display Indicators (DDIs) and the
Advanced Multipurpose Color Display (AMPCD) contain cold-start evidence that
is not always reducible to simple telemetry fields.

Third, assistance must be auditable. A natural-language answer is not enough
when it may direct the learner's next action. The system should preserve why
the answer was accepted, repaired, downgraded, or rejected. This motivates the
event-log and replay emphasis in later chapters. Fourth, assistance must be
bounded. A foundation model should not invent step identifiers, overlay
targets, source citations, or visual observations. Those elements must be
controlled by the procedure pack, source policy, schema validation, and
harness checks.

These four requirements explain the architecture of the thesis. Manuals and
retrieval provide textual authority. ITS and constraint-based tutoring provide
the need for explicit task state. Telemetry provides high-confidence simulator
variables. VLMs provide selected visual facts. The runtime binds them together
into an evidence packet before the language model is asked to generate an
explanation.

\section{Foundation Models as Bounded Components}\label{sec:bg-foundation}

Transformer-based foundation models provide the representational and
instruction-following basis for modern language and multimodal systems
\citep{vaswani2017attention}. In this thesis they are deliberately treated as
components, not as the whole tutor. The language model receives an evidence
packet and returns a structured response. The VLM receives an image and returns
a structured visual-fact object. Both outputs are checked before they can
affect user-facing guidance.

This component view changes the role of explanation. Explanation is not only a
piece of helpful prose. It is a checked relation among claims and evidence. If
the tutor says that a learner should continue a flight-control check, the
runtime should be able to identify which step is active, which preconditions
are satisfied, which retrieved source or procedure rule supports the guidance,
and whether the relevant visual or telemetry evidence is present. If that
evidence is missing, the model should not be allowed to fill the gap with a
confident story.

Human-AI interaction and explainable-AI work motivate this caution. Explainable
AI research emphasises the need to understand why a system produced a
recommendation and how such explanations should be evaluated
\citep{gunning2019xai,hoffman2018metrics}. Human-AI interaction and automation
research emphasise intelligibility, appropriate reliance, uncertainty
handling, and recoverability \citep{lee2004trust,amershi2019guidelines}.
The prototype operationalises these ideas in a narrow procedural setting: the
response schema, evidence-reference types, source policy, overlay allowlist,
and replay logs are the mechanism by which a fluent model answer becomes
inspectable.

\section{Visual Fact Extraction and PEFT Adaptation}\label{sec:bg-vlm}

Vision-language models extend foundation-model capabilities to image-text
tasks. General VLM work such as Qwen-VL, Qwen3-VL, LLaVA, and InstructBLIP
shows how visual encoders and language decoders can support image reasoning,
visual question answering, instruction following, and text-rich perception
\citep{bai2023qwen,qwen3vl2025,liu2023llava,dai2023instructblip}. The cockpit
task in this thesis uses these capabilities narrowly. The VLM is not asked for
a free-form scene description. It returns a typed JSON object over a fixed
visual-fact ontology.

The visual fact representation has three states:
\texttt{seen}, \texttt{not\_seen}, and \texttt{uncertain}. A fact such as
\texttt{fcsmc\_final\_go\_result\_visible} is a local claim about a visible
display cue. It is not a claim that the whole FCS BIT step is complete. That
distinction is central. The initial 8-fact ontology contained more compound
targets and exposed a recurring failure mode: the model could report a
procedure-completion-like fact as \texttt{seen} even when the human label was
\texttt{not\_seen}. The current 13-fact ontology decomposes such targets into
local visual atoms that the downstream procedure engine can interpret.

This design relates to screen and GUI understanding. Screen-content models and
UI datasets emphasise fixed regions, symbolic text, and layout rather than
ordinary natural images \citep{deka2017rico,wang2021screen2words}. Cockpit
displays share that structured quality. A small visual cue may be a pushbutton
label, a page title, a row in a BIT result table, or a transient alignment
message. The ontology therefore acts as a bridge between perception and
control logic.

\subsection{Structured Output and Reliability Metrics}\label{sec:bg-metrics}

The VLM task is structured prediction over a small ontology. For each image,
the model predicts a vector of categorical states rather than a caption. The
JSON format is the integration interface, and JSON Schema provides the formal
validation contract used to reject malformed or out-of-ontology outputs
\citep{pezoa2016jsonschema}. Still, the learning problem is not merely JSON
generation. The model must align visual evidence with fact
definitions, distinguish page labels from actual pages, separate transient
intermediate states from final states, and decide when a cue is too ambiguous
for a \texttt{seen} or \texttt{not\_seen} decision.

The evaluation therefore uses several metrics. JSON validity and schema
validity measure whether the model returned a usable object. Fact accuracy
counts individual fact-state decisions. Macro \(F_1\) balances state classes
across facts. Seen \(F_1\) focuses on positive visual detections. Sample exact
match asks whether the entire visual state vector is correct. Critical false
positives count high-risk cases where the model predicts \texttt{seen} for a
fact that should not be considered visible. This last metric matters because a
false \texttt{seen} label on a completion-like cue can corrupt downstream
procedure reasoning more severely than a missed cue that merely triggers
another check. This explicit metric mix follows broader classification-
evaluation work showing that accuracy, \(F_1\), and macro metrics answer
different questions and require task-specific justification
\citep{powers2011evaluation,opitz2024metrics}.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}\label{sec:bg-lora}

Full fine-tuning of large multimodal models is often unnecessary or
impractical for narrow domain tasks. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) freezes the
base model weights and learns low-rank update matrices for selected modules
\citep{hu2021lora}. If a base weight matrix \(W\) is frozen, LoRA represents
the update as:

\[
  W' = W + \Delta W,\qquad \Delta W = BA,
\]

where \(A\) and \(B\) are low-rank trainable matrices. Parameter-efficient
fine-tuning libraries and tooling such as PEFT, TRL, and Unsloth make this
workflow practical in local experiments
\citep{mangrulkar2022peft,vonwerra2020trl,unsloth2024}. Related PEFT variants
such as QLoRA and VeRA show that the field contains multiple ways to reduce
adaptation cost or parameter count \citep{dettmers2023qlora,kopiczko2023vera}.
This thesis does not propose a new PEFT algorithm. It uses LoRA as a practical
mechanism for domain-specific structured visual-fact adaptation.

The claim is therefore not ``a better LoRA method''. The claim is narrower:
for a VR procedural tutor, structured visual facts plus small-domain LoRA
adaptation can produce a more reliable evidence channel than using the base
VLM directly. This makes ontology design, label review, holdout independence,
and critical-false-positive analysis part of the ML method. The evaluated
Qwen3.5, Qwen3.6, and Gemma lines in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} are used to
show that model choice is a downstream-risk decision, not a leaderboard-only
decision.

The thesis sits between ML adaptation, tutoring systems, simulator
instrumentation, and HCI/VR evaluation. Its central contribution is not that it
invented manuals, telemetry, RAG, VLMs, LoRA, or AR/VR guidance. The
contribution is the disciplined integration: in a high-load VR procedural
training setting, a foundation model can provide auditable interactive
explanation only when telemetry, procedure rules, retrieval, visual facts,
validation, and replay logs constrain what the model is allowed to say and do.
No prior line surveyed above jointly provides all of these requirements for
the present case: live simulator-state evidence, multimodal cockpit facts,
procedure-level authority, bounded foundation-model generation, headset-facing
guidance, and replayable audit logs.
